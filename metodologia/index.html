Methodology

How we build road-trip budgets

By Editorial RoadTripRoutes · Last reviewed September 18, 2026

TL;DR

Every budget starts with official sources: AAA for gas, NPS and state-park pages for entry fees, state toll authorities for tolls, and USDOT speed-and-distance data for drive times. Figures are always dated, always linked, and labeled estimate-vs-fact. Below are the rules, a worked example, and the honest limits.

The one-line summary

“Plan the drive, not just the destination” works because legs turn a vague trip into a countable one. Each guide splits the route into legs and answers, for every leg, four questions that matter: how far, how long, what it costs, and what can go wrong. This page is the rulebook both we and you hold us to.

The sources we cite

We use official or primary public sources wherever one exists, and we say so when there isn’t a single authoritative number for something (lodging being the main case).

  • AAA — fuel prices

    AAA national & state gas price averages. Gas is the leg line item that moves fastest; we take the state average for the states a leg crosses, record the date, and link it. When a guide assumes a vehicle’s miles-per-gallon, the assumed MPG is printed next to the calculation.

  • National Park Service — entrance fees & passes

    NPS entrance fees and the America the Beautiful pass. We print the fee, the “as of” date and whether a pass would change the math. Where a guide crosses a state park or national forest, we use that agency’s official page instead.

  • State DOTs & toll authorities — tolls

    Toll figures come from the agency that sets them — for example Bay Area FasTrak for the bridges you’ll cross heading out of San Francisco. We flag the practical facts nobody puts in the SERP: which structures are toll-only, which have no cash lane, and what a rental-car transponder fee does to the total.

  • USDOT / FHWA — speed limits and distances

    Distances come from route legs we measure, and drive-time math uses posted speed limits via USDOT/FHWA data. We then add a realistic stop allowance — and we label a segment “slow and scenic” when the road is deliberately, gloriously unhurried.

  • Official road-closure & season pages

    Closures, fire season, bridge work and storm damage are checked on the owning agency’s own page (the relevant NPS or state DOT site) and dated on the guide, because “as of” is the only honest tense for a road closure.

The rules we can be held to

  1. Every figure is dated or labeled. A number without an “as of” date is an estimate, and it says so in the text.
  2. Official source first. If AAA, NPS, a DOT or a toll authority publishes it, we link them rather than a blog that links them.
  3. Ranges beat false precision. Lodging and seasonal gas are bands, not exact tickets. $140–$240 says more than $197.33 ever could.
  4. Assumptions are printed. The MPG we assume, the hours we drive per day, the stop allowance — all public, all contestable.
  5. One “what nobody tells you” per route. Each guide carries an original, sourced detail the search-result pages skip.
  6. No selling the answer. We never adjust a number to please a brand, a hotel or an ad buyer — the editorial guidelines make that a rule, not a value.

The numbered stop map

Every guide orders its stops in true driving order as a numbered list — stop 1 is the first place you pull over, not the prettiest one. It reads like a route, because it is one.

    Your first pull-over of the coastal primer: beach access and lunch before the coast road unrolls.

  2. Monterey & Carmel

    Day 1, afternoon

    Overnight base for the middle leg; the historic waterfront and the village of Carmel (public parking fills early).

  3. Big Sur & the Bixby Bridge

Plan ahead

On Highway 1 between Monterey and Big Sur, months of no-passing zones mean the maps’ “31 miles” pedals slower than the number suggests — budget the scenery. That is the kind of note every “what nobody tells you” flag in our guides is made of: original, specific, and true on the ground.

What an estimate can’t do

No budget survives first contact with a real road, and ours don’t pretend otherwise. We do not price flights, we do not quote fuel from a single truck stop, and we treat lodging as a band because a night’s rate is not a fact until you book it. Weather, fires, traffic and the last-minute whim to stay an extra night are yours — and so is the joy in them.

Our job is to make the trip countable before you go, with numbers you can trace to a source you can check. Nothing on RoadTripRoutes is a promise about what you will pay; it is a serious, dated, sourced estimate of what the drive costs. Verify current rates on the official sources before you travel.

Road glossary used on this site

Terms we use in the guides, defined like a field notebook rather than a dictionary.

Leg
A defined stretch of a route between two planned stops — the unit every budget and stop map is built from.
America the Beautiful pass
The annual pass covering entrance fees at 2,000+ federal recreation sites; we note where it changes a route’s math.
Posted limit
The legal speed limit for a segment; the basis of our pure drive time before stops are added.
Hard toll / toll-only
A crossing with no free alternative and frequently no cash lane — paid by plate or transponder, sometimes with a rental-car surcharge.
Slow and scenic
Our label for a segment where the road itself is the attraction — no-passing stretches, ocean cliffs, cloverleaf views.
What nobody tells you
The original, sourced detail in every guide that the search-result pages skip — the toll-only bridge, the cash-only lot, the mile of quarters you didn’t anticipate.

Review & update cadence

Guides are re-checked at least twice a year against the same sources, and sooner when a known change lands (park-fee increases, bridge toll revisions, permanent closures). Every change is logged with a new “last reviewed” date. Spotted a stale figure? Tell us — corrections are handled per the editorial guidelines.

Methodology FAQ

How do you calculate gas costs?

Gas per leg = miles ÷ assumed miles-per-gallon (printed beside the math) × the AAA state-average fuel price for the states the leg crosses, dated and linked. We never invent a per-gallon price; when a worked example uses a round number we label it an example.

Where do park entrance fees come from?

From the current NPS fee pages or the owning state agency, with the fee, the check date and the link, plus a note whenever the America the Beautiful pass would change the math.

How do you estimate lodging?

As a nightly price band from current public listings — official visitor bureaus and published booking data, not a single scraped deal. Bands beat false precision because a night’s rate isn’t a fact until you book it.

Do budgets include food, attractions and extras?

Leg tables cover the trip-critical lines — gas, tolls, entry fees, lodging. Food, tours and contingency cash appear as a separate per-day allowance, because tastes differ far more than fuel prices do.

How are drive times estimated?

Distance ÷ posted limit (USDOT/FHWA data) plus a stop allowance of roughly 15 minutes per two hours, with “slow and scenic” segments flagged where the road itself dictates the pace.

Why do figures go out of date?

Because prices, fees and tolls change. Every figure carries the date it was verified and every guide shows a “last reviewed” date; we re-check at least twice a year. Please verify current rates on the official sources before you travel.

What do you mean by “what nobody tells you”?

Each route gives you one original, sourced detail the SERP skips — the toll-only bridge, the no-cash-lane plaza, the day-use fee that appears when you’re already parked. It’s reporting, not recycled listicle filler.

Accuracy & affiliation

We cite NPS, AAA, USDOT and state agencies as sources; we are not affiliated with them and they do not endorse us. Figures on this page and in our guides are for general planning and are not a guarantee of current pricing — always confirm with the official source before you travel.
