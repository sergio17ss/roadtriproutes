Route 66 in seven days: the leg-by-leg budget

Home → Routes → Route 66 · 7-day guide

By Editorial RoadTripRoutes · Last reviewed September 25, 2026

Prices verified September 2026 Published in the centennial year

TL;DR

Drive Route 66 westbound from Chicago to Santa Monica in seven days: roughly 2,300 miles of signed historic alignments served by eight state departments of transportation. With 2026 fuel prices near record highs, budget ~$1,270–$1,960 for two people (gas, national-park entry and seven nights of lodging) before food and museums, using AAA state averages as of September 2026. There are zero tolls on the route itself. 2026 is the road’s centennial — the National Park Service frames the corridor at about 2,400 miles — so expect crowds at the big stops, and know the desert days run long.

Key facts

  • Route: Chicago (Jackson Blvd & Michigan Ave) → Springfield, IL → St. Louis → Joplin → Oklahoma City → Amarillo → Albuquerque → Flagstaff → Barstow → Santa Monica.
  • Miles: ~2,300 of signed driving over the seven days; the corridor spans eight states and about 2,448 miles in the highway logs (NPS rounds to about 2,400).
  • Days / pace: 7 nights; roughly five hours of pure driving a day, with Days 6–7 (Arizona and California) the long ones.
  • Budget: ~$1,270–$1,960 trip-critical for two — gas (~$413), one park pass ($25) and lodging only. No food, no museum tickets.
  • Tolls: $0 on Route 66. The only optional one is the $8.10 Chicago Skyway (2026) if you take I-90 out of the metro.
  • Best months: April–June and September–October, when the desert is temperate and the centennial events are spread across the calendar.
  • The catch: it is a driving trip in seven days, not a holiday of long lunches — and the record September 2026 gas market is the invisible surcharge on every mile.

The drive, at a glance

Route 66 — U.S. Highway 66, the “Mother Road” — ran from the shores of Lake Michigan to the Pacific, and the National Park Service puts the corridor at about 2,400 miles across Illinois, Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona and California. The original U.S. number was decommissioned in 1985, but the alignments were never bulldozed into nothing: roughly 85 percent of the old road remains drivable, kept signed and paved by the Illinois Department of Transportation, MoDOT, Oklahoma DOT, TxDOT, NMDOT, ADOT and Caltrans, and preserved by the NPS Route 66 Corridor Preservation Program. This guide runs the classic seven-day version westbound, Chicago to Santa Monica, priced leg by leg per how we build budgets (distance figures are our own leg measurements).

  • Route: Chicago → Springfield, IL → Chain of Rocks/St. Louis → Joplin → Oklahoma City → Amarillo → Albuquerque → Petrified Forest → Flagstaff → Barstow → Santa Monica Pier.
  • Miles: ~2,300 of daily legs, including loops like the 28-mile Petrified Forest park road and Oatman’s mountain switchbacks.
  • Days: 7 nights, ~3.5–5 hours of pure driving a day, five to eight hours with stops.
  • Budget: ~$1,270–$1,960 trip-critical for two — gas, the Petrified Forest pass and lodging, no food, no museum tickets.
  • Tolls: $0 on the route. The famous one is the one you never touch — read the toll reality.
  • Best months: April–June and September–October; summer is fine but the Mojave runs hot and the centennial-year parking lots fill by noon.
  • The occasion: 2026 is Route 66’s 100th birthday, celebrated state by state — from the Illinois end to the Pacific. A great year to go, and a crowded one.

Plan ahead

The fuel bill is the whole game in 2026. AAA’s national average hit $4.48 a gallon on September 24, 2026 — the highest for that point in the calendar ever — after crude climbed past $100 a barrel on Persian Gulf volatility. State averages split west to east: about $6.20 in California against about $3.95 in Texas and $4.05 in Oklahoma. Two easy hedges: fill the tank in the cheapest state your day crosses, and never cross California empty — it is the most expensive fuel on the continent right now.

Why the toll column reads zero

U.S. Highway 66, born in 1926 and officially decommissioned in 1985, crosses eight states and exactly no toll structures. The famous exception is the one that is optional: the Chicago Skyway (I-90) charges $8.10 for two-axle vehicles in 2026, up 30 cents, if you take that expressway east or south across the metro — and the Illinois Tollway (I-PASS/E-ZPass) system adds tolls only on its own interstates, never on the surface streets that carry Route 66 through the state. Skip those and this trip is genuinely toll-free, which is the rarest thing a long American drive has to offer. Full detail in the toll reality below.

Park & entry. The only required fee on this itinerary is Petrified Forest National Park at $25 per private vehicle (cashless, valid seven days) on Day 6 — the park road is the old corridor’s neighbor and the fee is unavoidable if you drive it. The optional Day 6 canyon detour is Grand Canyon at $35 per vehicle. If you plan to hit both, the $80 America the Beautiful pass nets out after just two fee parks — and it also waives the $100-per-person nonresident surcharge some of the 11-strong list of parks, including Grand Canyon, began charging on January 1, 2026.

Lodging. Bands come from current public listings along the corridor, per our rule. Springfield and Amarillo are the cheap nights; Santa Monica is the expensive one; the centennial-year bookings pushed Springfield and Flagstaff up modestly. Most drivers simplify by booking the two anchor cities (Oklahoma City, Albuquerque) well ahead, then letting the roadside motels ride.

What the table does not hold. Food runs roughly $35–$60 per person per day on this route — Route 66 remains a diner-and-drive-thru highway, which is part of its economy. Museums are cheap and optional: the Route 66 Hall of Fame & Museum in Pontiac is a small admission, the California Route 66 museums run a few dollars, and most roadside icons are free. Add them where they matter, not where a listicle says they must go.

Plan ahead — the two long days

Days 6 and 7 are the ones that humble a party. Day 6 works if you enter Petrified Forest at the north entrance, drive the 28-mile park road once, and leave through Rainbow Forest — it is a through-road, not an out-and-back. Day 7 is the brute: 460 miles across the Arizona desert, into the Mojave, and through Los Angeles traffic to the pier. Leave Flagstaff no later than 7 a.m., do the Oatman loop early, and know that Santa Monica is the destination but Barstow is the deadline — if you are not through the Cajon Pass by 3 p.m., the last two hours double.

The seven days, in order

Day 1 — Chicago to Springfield, Illinois (200 mi)

Begin at the corner the NPS calls the starting point of the Mother Road: Jackson Boulevard at Michigan Avenue, on the lakeshore. The first 60 miles are Chicago itself — Ogden Avenue now carries the city’s new 2026 wayfinding signs — then the road opens into Joliet and Wilmington, home of the 28-foot Gemini Giant, a classic muffler-man statue. Pontiac holds the genuinely great Route 66 Hall of Fame & Museum, and Springfield closes the day at the Shea’s Gas Station Museum and the centennial-flavored State Fairgrounds. September’s International Route 66 Mother Road Festival runs September 25–27, 2026 in Springfield — if your dates line up, expect town-wide crowds and book the room months out. This is the easiest day of the trip, and the only one with real city traffic at both ends, so use it to calibrate the pace: this trip is a drive, and the sooner the group agrees, the better.

Day 2 — Springfield, Illinois to Joplin, Missouri (300 mi)

Morning begins with the Illinois prairie rolling past Funk’s Grove, then the day’s landmark moment at St. Louis: the old road crossed the Mississippi on the Chain of Rocks Bridge, now a bicycle-and-walking span you can drive only in memory — the car route heads over a modern bridge and spends an hour in the city. Past the metro, Route 66 is rural again: Cuba, Rolla and Lebanon thread through the Missouri Ozarks, where Route 66 State Park marks the alignment’s old river crossing and the two-lane bends the way the motorists of 1930 knew it. Overnight at Springfield, Missouri or push on to Joplin, both cheap nights with motels on the old business routes that MoDOT keeps signed. Fuel is mid-range in Missouri (~$4.20), so this is a fill-up-here morning, not a hold-out-for-cheaper.

Day 3 — Joplin to Oklahoma City (340 mi)

The day starts with the shortest, strangest state on the route: Kansas. Route 66’s 13-mile thumb dips through Galena and Baxter Springs on its way into Oklahoma, and Galena’s Cars on the Route showroom is the whole-state stop. Then Oklahoma City pulls you down the full length of the state’s 432-mile share — the largest rural section of the corridor, per Oklahoma DOT’s Route 66 program: the Coleman Theatre at Miami, Will Rogers at Claremore, the collection of neon at Tulsa, and finally Oklahoma City itself, whose National Route 66 Museum at El Reno and Route 66 Park anchor the corridor’s state capital. Overnight OKC at its cheap-lantern hotels. Watch the clock: with the Ozarks behind you, Oklahoma’s 70 mph county stretches finally let you bank miles.

Day 4 — Oklahoma City to Amarillo (290 mi)

West of OKC the road gives up its last pretense of scenery and becomes what it always was — the fastest, straightest line west. Clinton’s Oklahoma Route 66 Museum is the morning’s best stop, then the run through Weatherford and Elk City to the Texas line. Texas is only 186 miles of Route 66, but the Texas Panhandle section is the most famous road sign on the whole trip: Shamrock’s art-deco tower, McLean’s Phillips 66, the tilted Leaning Tower of Groom, and Amarillo’s own Cadillac Ranch, ten buried cars you are legally allowed to spray-paint. Texas has the cheapest gas on the trip (about $3.95) — this is the fill-up day, and the reason the fuel bill starts dropping here. Overnight Amarillo is the budget night of the trip.

Day 5 — Amarillo to Albuquerque (290 mi)

Adrian, Texas, 20 miles past Amarillo, holds the mathematical center of Route 66 — the Midpoint Cafe where east meets west — and the state line mirror of it in New Mexico minutes later. Then the corridor’s most loved relic: Tucumcari, a town of preserved neon motels (the Blue Swallow among them) that NMDOT keeps on the historic map, followed by Santa Rosa’s Blue Hole. New Mexico claims the longest single-state share of the modern corridor at 487 miles, and by late afternoon you crest Tijeras Pass into Albuquerque, where Old Town and the Sandias give the trip its first genuinely mountain night. Gas costs more in New Mexico (about $4.45) than in Texas — another reason Day 4’s cheap fill matters.

Day 6 — Albuquerque to Flagstaff via Petrified Forest (380 mi)

The big day. From Albuquerque, Grants and Gallup slide past, and the state line at Lupton tips you into Arizona, whose 401 miles include the corridor’s single required entrance fee: Petrified Forest National Park, $25 per vehicle, entered at the north Painted Desert visitor center and exited south at Rainbow Forest after the 28-mile park road. It is not a detour so much as the road itself — the historic alignment runs beside the park, and the drive-through is the fastest way west. Back on the highway, Holbrook’s wigwam motel and Winslow’s “Standin’ on the Corner” park are ten-minute photo stops, and Flagstaff closes the day at 7,000 feet, cool even in September. The optional add-on — if you have one flexible afternoon — is the Grand Canyon South Rim ($35 per vehicle) from Williams, and note the NPS reopened the North Rim on May 15, 2026 after the 2025 Dragon Bravo Fire; services are still stripped back there, so the South Rim is the sensible one for a single day.

Day 7 — Flagstaff to Santa Monica (460 mi)

The brute day, and the payoff. Williams, the last Arizona town to kill its interstate bypass signs, then Seligman (where the roll-your-own historic alignments begin), then Kingman, where the road splits: the modern run rides I-40 past the Mojave, and the romantic run drops south to Oatman, a burro-populated ghost town reached by the highway’s steepest switchbacks. Rejoining west, the desert tightens into Barstow, Victorville and the Cajon Pass, then Los Angeles traffic to Pasadena, downtown, and the terminus the NPS calls the end of the trail: Santa Monica Pier, where the Mother Road meets the Pacific. California’s fuel at about $6.20 makes this the priciest leg by far — cross the Arizona line with a full tank and treat the state’s pumps as a last-resort. Leave at dawn; this is the day that decides the whole trip’s mood.

What nobody tells you: the tolls you don’t pay (and the one you might)

The internet assumes Route 66 is a toll road. It was born in 1926 as a free national highway and it is still a toll-free one: the historic alignments maintained by IDOT, MoDOT, Oklahoma DOT, TxDOT, NMDOT, ADOT and Caltrans carry no toll plazas. The only tolls that can reach you are the ones you add by choosing the wrong first 30 miles or the wrong LA approach.

The Chicago exit. The historic road leaves town on surface streets, toll-free. But if your rental drop-off or your route logic puts you on the Chicago Skyway (I-90), it charges $8.10 for a two-axle vehicle in 2026 — raised 30 cents on January 1 — payable by cash, card or any E-ZPass/I-PASS tag. The Illinois Tollway only appears on its own interstates (I-88, I-294, I-355 and friends), none of which you need for Route 66; avoid both and the toll column stays empty for 2,300 miles.

Three more notes the search pages regularly skip, each verified and dated:

  • The “66” signs are state-owned. After decommissioning, the shields came up as state historic-route signage under the Federal Highway Administration’s byway framework; the familiar black-and-white “Historic Route 66” markers are printed by the states and highway associations, not the federal government.
  • Petrified Forest is cashless. Park by park, NPS is dropping cash; Petrified Forest accepts only credit/debit, as does Grand Canyon. Card or America the Beautiful pass or you are turning around.
  • Nonresident park surcharges arrived in 2026. As of January 1, 2026, eleven national parks — Grand Canyon among them — add $100 per person for non-U.S. residents 16 and over, on top of the entrance fee, unless covered by an Annual or America the Beautiful pass. International drivers should buy the pass before the gate.

Seasons, gas prices and the centennial circus

Route 66 is a four-season road held together by one weather reality: the Mojave and Chihuahuan deserts are June–August ovens, and the Illinois and Missouri legs are genuinely cold by late November. April–June and September–October give the balance: warm pavements, clear Ozark and canyon skies, and motel rates below the July peak. The corridor’s two elevations — Flagstaff at 7,000 feet and the desert floors around 2,000 — can be 30–40°F apart in the same afternoon, so the layers rule applies.

The 2026 wildcard is the centennial itself. The NPS Route 66 Corridor Preservation Program, created by Congress in 1999, has plowed more than 170 cost-share grants and 250-plus National Register listings into the corridor since 2001 — and the 100th year is the busiest yet, with Illinois’ commission-led events calendar and a new IDOT centennial map released in March 2026. Expect fuller parking lots at the icons, higher motel demand in Springfield and Flagstaff, and slower photo queues all summer and early fall. There is also a genuine preservation warning in the data: the NPS re-surveys of the corridor found an average 25 percent loss of historic buildings across a 20-year window — the reason the program’s technical assistance matters more in its centennial year than ever.

What changed in 2026

Gas hit the highest prices ever recorded for September — the AAA national average reached $4.48 on September 24, 2026 with crude above $100 — pushing this route’s fuel line past $400 for the first time. The Grand Canyon North Rim reopened May 15, 2026 after the 2025 Dragon Bravo Fire (day-use only, no overnight lodging). And Route 66 turned 100 on November 11, 2026, a state-by-state celebration the Illinois Route 66 Centennial Commission has been running since February.

The verdict

Seven days is the minimum honest version of Route 66 — enough to stop, enough to stay, and exactly the length where the road remains the point. The numbers are the 2026 reality: about 2,300 miles, roughly five hours of pure driving a day, a record-priced fuel bill near $413, $25 in required park entry, zero tolls, and a $830–$1,520 lodging spread for the week. For two adults in one car that is ~$1,270–$1,960 trip-critical, which is remarkably cheap for eight states and the most famous highway in America.

Take it slower — two to three weeks — if you want the Ozark chairs and the evening on the Santa Monica beach to carry the week. Take it faster than seven days and you are driving the interstate shadow of the route, which defeats the entire reason to go. And go west — the Pacific termination of the Mother Road is the ending the road was always heading for, and it is the one that makes the tank full of California gas worth it. Route 66 FAQ

How much does a seven-day Route 66 trip cost?

For two adults in one car, the trip-critical lines are ~$1,270–$1,960: roughly $413 in gas (about 2,300 miles at 26 mpg across AAA’s eight state averages of September 2026, from about $3.95 in Texas to about $6.20 in California), $25 in park entry (Petrified Forest), zero tolls, and $830–$1,520 for seven nights of lodging. Food ($35–$60/person/day), small museum admissions and the optional $35 Grand Canyon pass sit outside that range.

Which direction should I drive Route 66?

Westbound, Chicago to Santa Monica. Ending at Santa Monica Pier is the official western terminus and the emotional payoff of the road. The solar trade: heading west puts the afternoon sun ahead of you. Early starts neutralize it, and westbound remains the direction the highway was designed to run and the one every serious guidebook defaults to.

Are there any tolls on Route 66 in 2026?

On the historic highway, no. Route 66 crosses eight states with zero toll plazas. The only tolls that can reach you are the optional $8.10 Chicago Skyway if you take I-90 out of the metro and any Illinois Tollway segments you add on its interstates. Skip both and the toll column is empty for the whole trip.

How many days do you need to drive Route 66?

Two to three weeks is ideal, but seven days is the honest minimum for a real trip — about five hours of driving a day, with Days 6 and 7 the long ones. Fewer days reduces the route to a freeway corridor move, which any Route 66 purist — and the NPS preservation program’s whole reason for existing — will tell you is not the point of the road.

Is Route 66 still drivable in 2026?

Yes. The U.S. 66 designation was retired in 1985, but the alignments survive as a signed patchwork kept up by the eight state DOTs — IDOT, Oklahoma DOT, TxDOT, ADOT and the rest — and roughly 85 percent remains driveable. 2026 is the centennial, with new signage and events from the Illinois commission west to the California association down the line.

Do I need park passes for Petrified Forest and Grand Canyon?

Petrified Forest is $25 per vehicle (cashless, valid seven days) and sits on the corridor — you drive the 28-mile park road through it either way. The Grand Canyon South Rim is $35 per vehicle for the optional Day 6 detour. The $80 America the Beautiful pass covers both — which, for a route with two fee parks and eleven parks now charging a $100 nonresident surcharge, makes it the smart prepurchase for 2026.
