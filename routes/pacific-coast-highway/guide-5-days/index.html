Pacific Coast Highway in five days: the leg-by-leg budget

By Editorial RoadTripRoutes · Last reviewed September 24, 2026

Prices verified September 2026

TL;DR

Drive Highway 1 southbound from San Francisco to Santa Monica in five days: about 450 miles when you count the 17-Mile Drive loop. With 2026 fuel prices, budget ~$1,010–$1,610 for two people (gas, park and entry fees, and five nights of lodging) before food and extras, using AAA’s California average as of September 2026. There are no bridge tolls on the route itself. Big Sur is open again after three years of landslides, but wildfire closures returned in August–September 2026 — check Caltrans QuickMap the morning you leave.

The drive, at a glance

The Pacific Coast Highway — formally California State Route 1, a designated All-American Road under the FHWA National Scenic Byways program — is the cliff-hugging California coastline made drivable. This guide runs the classic southbound five-day version: San Francisco to Santa Cruz, Monterey and Carmel, the Big Sur coast, Cambria and San Simeon, Morro Bay and Pismo, and down to Santa Barbara, Malibu and Santa Monica. That is the roughly 450-mile route Visit California points people toward when it plans 4–7 days along the coast, and every guide we rely on treats it as the blockbuster American drive (distance figures in this article are our own leg measurements, per how we build budgets).

  • Route: Highway 1 south, San Francisco → Santa Cruz → Monterey/Carmel → Big Sur → Cambria/San Simeon → Santa Barbara → Santa Monica (US-101 south of Pismo Beach).
  • Miles: ~450 total, including the ~17-loop that is the 17-Mile Drive (folded into Day 2).
  • Days: 5 nights, roughly two hours of pure driving a day and three and a half to five hours with stops.
  • Budget: ~$1,010–$1,610 trip-critical for two — that is gas, fees and lodging, no food, no tickets.
  • Tolls: $0 on the route. The famous bridge toll is the one you won’t pay — read the toll reality.
  • Best months: May–June and September–October, with the fog usually gone by lunch and the summer crowds thinned out.
  • The catch: fire season. Highway 1 reopened all the way through Big Sur in January 2026, then closed again during the August–September 2026 wildfires. Conditions change within hours — treat this itinerary as a plan, not a guarantee.

Plan ahead

Big Sur is the seam that governs your whole drive. Caltrans reopened the Regent’s Slide corridor in January 2026 after almost three years of slides, fixing the last gap between Carmel and Cambria — the first time the entire route had been passable in years. But wildfire closures came back in force: the Timber and Plaskett fires shut stretches of Highway 1 repeatedly in August and September 2026, and the Big Sur state parks were still closed at publication. Check Caltrans QuickMap, California State Parks closures and Ready Monterey County the morning you leave, and read the fallback plan before you commit to that leg.

Why the toll column reads zero

Highway 1 heading south out of San Francisco crosses no toll bridges. If you fly into SFO or stay in the city, your first and last dollar stops at the rental counter. If instead you pick up a car near Oakland (OAK) or San Jose (SJC), expect one Bay Bridge toll of $8.50 westbound to get into the city — the six state-owned Bay Area bridges moved to all-electronic tolling, and the rate is the same with or without FasTrak. It is the only bridge fee this itinerary can charge you, and it is optional. Details in the toll reality below.

The formula, dated and linked

Gas. Cells use ~$6.00/gallon — the AAA California state average, which ran about $5.85 in early September 2026 and climbed past $6.00 by mid-September as crude spiked, against a national average near $4.43. That fuel figure is the single most volatile line in this budget; it can be off by $30–$40 by the time you read this. The math is printed so you can recompute it on the day: miles ÷ 26 mpg × today’s AAA California price.

Park & entry. The 17-Mile Drive gate is $12.50 a vehicle, refunded against a $35-or-more purchase at any Pebble Beach Resorts restaurant on the property. Pfeiffer Big Sur State Park day use is $10 a vehicle when the park is open — which, as of publication, it was not, due to the fires; plan on the $0 fallback and treat the $10 as the “scenery is back” add-back. Point Lobos, if you squeeze it into the morning, is $10 a vehicle.

Lodging. Bands come from current public listings rather than a single scraped deal, per our rule. Santa Cruz and Cambria run cheaper, Monterey/Carmel and Santa Barbara sit at the top. The September 2026 reopen surge pushed Big Sur region rooms to premium rates, which is one more reason this itinerary sleeps in Monterey and San Simeon/Cambria rather than inside Big Sur itself.

What the table does not hold. Food runs $50–$75 per person per day on this route if you eat normally and stay flexible. Tickets are optional and priced: Monterey Bay Aquarium $65 adult, Hearst Castle Grand Rooms $35 adult. Add them where they matter to you, not where a listicle tells you they must go.

Plan ahead — the Big Sur contingency

On the 95-mile Carmel-to-Cambria leg, “with stops,” you are choosing between Bixby Bridge, the Big Sur Valley, McWay Falls and the turn and taking most of a day. If a fire closure is active, Caltrans routes you over US-101 instead — east from Carmel to Salinas, then south past Paso Robles to rejoin Highway 1 at Cambria. It costs roughly 110 miles instead of 95 and drops the park fees to zero, but it also drops the entire reason for the leg. When the road is closed, the honest move is to spend a third day in the Monterey/Carmel/coast area and let Big Sur wait for a weekend when Caltrans says it is open.

The five days, in order

Day 1 — San Francisco to Santa Cruz (75 mi)

Leave the city the way the ocean does — south. Highway 1 picks up at the western edge of San Francisco and runs a coastline of its own through the Pacifica and Half Moon Bay surf towns, past the Devila Slide’s tunnels that replaced the old cliff road, and along a shore you can almost touch from the shoulder. Fifty miles down, Pigeon Point shows up as the Coastside’s white-and-red beacon, a strong first stretch-your-legs stop, with the elephant-seal colony of Año Nuevo State Reserve a little farther on (viewing is seasonal, typically winter through spring). Then the road opens onto the broad sweep of Monterey Bay and Santa Cruz — the wharf, the gingerbread-feeling boardwalk, the surf at Pleasure Point. It is the least “Highway 1 is a cliff” day of the trip, and that is the point: Sunday-driving flat-out before the road starts to climb.

Day 2 — Santa Cruz to Monterey and Carmel (48 mi + the 17-Mile loop)

The morning’s short hop follows the rim of Monterey Bay past mossy bluffs into Monterey. Cannery Row is now more souvenir than sardine, but the Monterey Bay Aquarium ($65 adult) remains the single best-spent ticket on the northern half of the drive if you have the hours — book it online; the kelp-forest galleries are the payoff. From Pacific Grove, the 17-Mile Drive (gate $12.50, refunded against a $35-or-more lunch or dinner at a Pebble Beach Resorts restaurant) loops past the Lone Cypress and the sea stacks, then lands you in Carmel-by-the-Sea, where the streets run to the beach and the night-town is walkable. Sleep Monterey if the budget needs a break; tasteful Carmel is the more charming bet if it does not.

Day 3 — Carmel, Big Sur and the turn (95 mi to Cambria/San Simeon)

This is the day you came for. Ten miles past Carmel you get Point Lobos ($10 a vehicle; its small lot fills by mid-morning on fine weekends) and then, at mile 13, Bixby Creek Bridge, the 260-foot-high arch that is the single most photographed structure on the road. Past Hurricane Point the coast closes in, and the next thirty miles — the Big Sur Valley, Pfeiffer Canyon, the redwood-shrouded Pfeiffer Big Sur State Park, and the McWay Falls lookout at Julia Pfeiffer Burns — is the stretch Slow and scenic was invented for. Then the road unrolls 40 more miles past Lucia and Gorda to San Simeon, where Hearst’s hilltop rises out of the chaparral, and you sleep in the quiet coast towns of San Simeon or Cambria. Standard advice, but printed anyway: top the tank and download offline maps in Carmel. There is no guarantee of fuel or cell signal on this leg, and the coast has shut before without a calendar warning.

Day 4 — San Simeon to Santa Barbara via Morro Bay and Pismo (120 mi)

Start with the 5-mile bus ride up Hearst Castle ($35 adult for the Grand Rooms tour, booking strongly recommended, arrive 20 minutes early) or park at the free elephant-seal rookery just north of it — one of the largest mainland colonies in the world, best seen in the fall when the bulls come ashore. Then follow the coast past Cambria, hug Morro Bay and its volcanic rock, cross the sand dunes at Pismo Beach, and turn inland on US-101 for the last hour to Santa Barbara — Mission Santa Barbara, State Street, and a long sunset with your feet off the gas. This is the day that feels like the holiday begins.

Day 5 — Santa Barbara to Malibu and Santa Monica (95 mi)

The final leg drops down the Rincon coast through Ventura, then onto the real Pacific Coast Highway street run — cliffside Malibu, the beach towns of the Palisades, and the state’s favorite ending, Santa Monica Pier. Two honest warnings: this is the busiest lane of the drive, and traffic toward Santa Monica gets thick after three or four in the afternoon, so either leave Santa Barbara early or let the pier be a morning-of-day-six errand. It is also the part most parties bolt through on the way north; taking the hour to stop in Malibu keeps the ending from feeling like a freeway commute back into the city you already left.

What nobody tells you: the tolls you don’t pay (and the one you might)

The internet assumes you will cross the Golden Gate. You won’t. Every popular search result piles “Golden Gate Bridge toll” onto the Pacific Coast Highway budget without noticing that the bridge charges southbound traffic arriving into San Francisco — $10.25 with FasTrak, $10.50 by license plate, $11.25 by mailed invoice since July 1, 2026 — and that a southbound exit from the city puts the bridge in your rear window, never ahead of you. The PCH proper, San Francisco to Santa Monica, crosses exactly zero toll structures.

The toll that can actually reach you is the one at the start: if you land at Oakland or San Jose and drive across a Bay Area bridge to collect your car or your hotel, the Bay Bridge toll is $8.50 westbound, same with or without a FasTrak tag, on the seven state bridges that moved to all-electronic collection. And here is the rental-car kicker the SERP skips: many agencies enroll their fleets in toll-by-plate programs, so a single $8.50 crossing can come back with a service fee on top of the toll, and cash is not an option at any of these plazas. The cheap move is to decline the agency pass, carry your own FasTrak tag or pay the plate invoice yourself from FasTrak’s site within two days. It is the single most useful $8.50 worth of planning on this route — and it belongs in the “if you start across the bay” column, not in every leg of the drive.

Three more notes the search pages regularly skip, each verified and dated:

  • The 17-Mile Drive refunds its own gate fee. The $12.50 entry is credited back with a $35-or-more purchase at Pebble Beach Resorts restaurants on the property. Eat the lunch there and your scenic loop costs nothing but the hour.
  • Point Lobos runs out of room. The reserve sits on a small day-use lot that fills early on fine mornings; there is walk-in parking along Highway 1 when it does, but your Day 3 morning is better spent arriving at open (8 a.m.), fee or no fee.
  • Bixby’s famous pull-off is now a target. After the 2026 reopen brought a surge of roadside parking, Monterey County moved to restrict parking around the bridge; don’t treat the turnout as a guaranteed spot, and never the active lane.

Seasons, fog and the fire wildcard

The coast works on its own thermostat. May through early June and September through October buy the most settled weather with the marine layer clearing by early afternoon and the summer crowds thinning; July and August trade perfect afternoons for full parking lots and premium room rates. Fog is not a mistake — it is the climate, and it makes the cliffs moodier rather than less. Winter is cheap and empty but the storms that feast on this coast are the ones that close it.

Which brings the honest asterisk: California fire season now reaches this road. In August and September 2026 the Timber and Plaskett fires shut stretches of Highway 1 between the Big Sur Valley and the San Luis Obispo county line, closed the Big Sur state parks, and sent the region’s reopening momentum into reverse. Caltrans, Cal Fire, the Los Padres National Forest and Monroe County run the live status; this article is a plan against the day you check it. That is standard, safe advice and it is mandatory on this road: verify the day-of picture, carry the US-101 fallback in your head, and never treat a dated “reopened” headline as a weather report.

What changed in 2026

Highway 1 reopened fully through Big Sur in January 2026 — the first time Carmel-to-Cambria had been continuously passable since the Regent’s and Paul’s Slides shut it years earlier. Then came the fires. As of late September 2026 the drive described above requires a same-day check of Caltrans QuickMap and California State Parks closures; go with the US-101 fallback if the seam is closed.

The verdict

Five days is the right amount of Highway 1 — enough to stop, enough to stay, and not a day more before the coast turns to routine. The driving math is lazier than people fear: about nine hours of pure pavement across the week, which is exactly why the 17-Mile Drive and the McWay overlook exist to push it toward fourteen hours with stops. Budget honestly, expect the fire-season adjustment to be the one true unknown, and remember that on this route you can park the skepticism at the first turnout.

Take the 3-day version only as a corridor move (you will eat windshield time and skip everything east of the road), and give yourself 7–8 days only if you plan to hammock in Big Sur or Santa Barbara for the sake of it. The 5-day, four-stop version is the Goldilocks one, and at ~$1,010–$1,610 trip-critical for two, it costs less than most people’s assumptions — because the toll column they budgeted for was a column they were never in. That, more than any scenery, is why we price this road leg by leg. Here is how the numbers are built.

Pacific Coast Highway FAQ

How much does a five-day Pacific Coast Highway trip cost?

For two adults in one car, the trip-critical lines come to ~$1,010–$1,610 for the five days: roughly $104 in gas (450 miles at 26 mpg on AAA’s California average of about $6.00/gallon, September 2026), zero bridge tolls on the route itself, $22.50 in park and entry fees, and five nights of lodging as a $880–$1,480 estimate band. Food ($50–$75/person/day), the Aquarium at $65 and Hearst Castle at $35 sit outside that range.

Is Highway 1 open through Big Sur right now?

It reopened fully in January 2026 after roughly three years of landslide closures, but wildfire closures returned when the Timber and Plaskett fires shut stretches repeatedly through August and September 2026. “Open” is a day-by-day status: check Caltrans QuickMap, state park closures and Ready Monterey County, and treat any headline older than 24 hours as stale.

Do I pay any tolls on the Pacific Coast Highway?

Driving south from San Francisco on Highway 1, no. The Golden Gate charges only southbound traffic entering the city ($10.25 FasTrak / $10.50 plate / $11.25 invoice since July 1, 2026) — on this route it sits behind you. The only toll that can reach you is the $8.50 Bay Bridge if you start on the East Bay side. Rental toll-by-plate programs can add processing fees on top of any crossing.

Which direction should I drive the Pacific Coast Highway?

Southbound, San Francisco to Santa Monica. Heading south keeps the Pacific on your right for nearly the whole route — pull-outs sit on your side instead of across traffic — and the afternoon sun lands behind you rather than in your mirrors. It is the direction Visit California and the itinerary community default to for those reasons.

When is the best time to drive the Pacific Coast Highway?

Late spring (May–June) and September–October give the most settled coastal weather with lighter crowds, and the marine fog usually clears by early afternoon. Fire season is the wildcard — the August–September 2026 closures are the demonstration — so pair a good calendar window with a same-morning check of Caltrans QuickMap before you leave.
