Editorial

Why we plan the drive

A destination is a pin on a map. The drive is the memory — the leg where you first saw the Pacific, the gas-station counter where the valley talk happened, the detour that became the story. Most guides tell you where to sleep and never tell you what the road costs. We think a trip starts when you can put numbers to the miles.

So each of our routes carries three things you can take to the bank: a leg-by-leg budget table, a numbered stop map in real driving order, and a “what nobody tells you” note — the overlooked detail that trips people up, from toll-only bridges to cash-only park lots.

— Editorial RoadTripRoutes

Good questions

Road-trip questions we actually get asked

How much should I budget per day for a US road trip?

For the drives we cover it commonly runs $150–$300 per day for two people on the road — gas, lodging and park fees. Buckets vary a lot by season and route, which is exactly why we publish leg-by-leg ranges with dated figures and links to official sources instead of a single “average road trip cost” number that means nothing to your trip.

Read the detail in how we calculate budgets.

What’s the best time of year for a coastal highway drive?

For west-coast coastal routes like the Pacific Coast Highway, late spring and early fall usually bring the most settled weather with the lightest summer crowds. Expect morning marine layer along the coast whatever the month — it usually clears by midday and is part of the coastline’s local character.

Every seasonal recommendation on this site is dated, and each guide flags what changes month to month — closures, fire season and shoulder-season lodging windows included.

Do I need a National Park pass, and is it worth it?

The America the Beautiful pass covers entrance fees at more than 2,000 federal recreation sites — national parks, forests, monuments and more. If your year holds two or more major park visits, it usually pays for itself within twelve months.

Entrance fee amounts quoted in our guides come from NPS official fee pages, are dated, and note wherever a park’s pass math would change the recommendation.

How many miles should I plan to drive in a day?

As a planning rule we use 150–250 miles per day with stops — the whole point of a road trip is the drive, not an endurance test. Our guides publish leg-by-leg drive times built from posted limits (from USDOT data) plus realistic stop allowances, rather than optimistic “it’s only four hours” shortcuts that never survive a restroom stop.

Where do the numbers on this site come from?

Every figure is sourced or honestly labeled:

  • Gas — AAA state-average fuel prices, dated and linked.
  • Park fees — NPS official entrance-fee pages.
  • Tolls — state toll authorities (for example, California’s FastTrak pages).
  • Drive times — posted speed limits and distances via USDOT data, plus realistic stops.

See the full methodology for the rules and the honest limits of any estimate.

How often are these guides updated?

Routes are reviewed at least twice a year, and more often when a park fee or a major segment changes. Every page carries a visible “last reviewed” date so you always know how current the numbers are — prices do change, and we’d rather you check than get caught out. Spot a stale number? Tell us — corrections are part of the editorial process.
